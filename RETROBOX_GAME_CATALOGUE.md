# RetroBox — Pre-Smartphone Mobile Game Research Catalogue

**Status:** Discovery draft v0.1  
**Purpose:** Build a research-backed list of memorable mobile games from the feature-phone era, identify which *types of gameplay* are practical to remake as original games, and choose a focused RetroBox launch library.

> **Working product idea:** RetroBox is an installable web-based retro gaming collection inspired by mobile games people played before Android and iOS became the dominant mobile platforms. It should recreate the feeling of playing small, immediately understandable games on old phones—not become a pile of unconnected clones.

---

## 1. Scope and research caveats

For this research, “pre-smartphone mobile games” primarily means games from roughly **1994–2008**, with some 2008–2010 Java ME/Symbian games included where they belong to the same feature-phone era. It includes Nokia firmware games, downloadable Java ME/J2ME games, Symbian/N-Gage titles, BlackBerry games, and selected Japanese i-mode/BREW examples.

There is no single authoritative list of “all popular games.” Popularity varied by country, handset, operator, and which games were preinstalled. Java ME alone has thousands of preserved titles—one current catalogue advertises more than 6,000—so this document is a **curated starting catalogue**, not a claim to have exhaustively ranked every release. A number of titles below are useful historical references but should not be treated as proposed V1 games.

The early platforms also had different distribution models. Snake was built into Nokia handsets; Java ME and Qualcomm BREW made downloadable games possible across multiple handsets; N-Gage provided a more dedicated gaming-phone experience. That means RetroBox should research both built-in classics and downloaded games.

### E. Japanese mobile and other regional catalogues — a second research pass

Japan's i-mode/i-appli ecosystem had titles and services that were less visible in Western catalogues. Examples include **Sonic Cafe**, **Space Harrier**, **Ridge Racer** and **Before Crisis: Final Fantasy VII**. Qualcomm BREW also powered downloadable games in some North American markets. These catalogues deserve a separate pass if we want to build a truly international archive rather than a mostly Nokia/Java nostalgia product.

## 3. Initial remake feasibility shortlist

Scores are **working estimates**, not measured facts. “Effort” means implementation effort for a good original 2D browser game, not effort to make an exact port. Nostalgia is a qualitative estimate based on historical visibility and recurring mentions in retro-game lists; user testing is needed to validate the audience we care about.

| Rank | Game concept to build (not original title) | Nostalgia potential | PWA feasibility | Effort | Why it is a candidate |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Physics ball platformer** (inspired by Bounce/Bounce Tales) | Very high | High | Medium | Recognisable character of play, side-scrolling levels, physics puzzles and room for original art/story |
| 2 | **Scrolling space shooter** (inspired by Space Impact/Siberian Strike) | High | Very high | Low–medium | Clear controls, short sessions, bosses, score chase and level packs are easy to understand |
| 3 | **Precision tower stacker** (inspired by Tower Bloxx) | High | Very high | Low | A compact game with a satisfying timing loop and low asset requirements |
| 4 | **Physics trials racer** (inspired by Gravity Defied) | High within Java-game fans | High | High | Distinctive and memorable, but vehicle physics will need extensive tuning |
| 5 | **Puzzle-adventure platformer** (inspired by Diamond Rush) | High within Java-game fans | High | Medium–high | Gives RetroBox a deeper game with levels, secrets, and progression |
| 6 | **Modern grid snake** (inspired by Snake/Snake II) | Very high | Very high | Very low | A natural demo and familiar entry point; needs a twist so it is not just another Snake clone |
| 7 | **Arcade brick breaker** (inspired by BrickBreaker/Block Breaker) | High | Very high | Low–medium | Straightforward to implement, tune and expand with original power-ups and stages |
| 8 | **Rapid reflex rolling game** (inspired by Rapid Roll) | Medium–high | Very high | Low–medium | Great for quick attempts, increasing speed and daily challenges |
| 9 | **Pocket pool or bowling** | Medium | High | Medium | Good controls-based arcade option; feel and physics matter |
| 10 | **Turn-based mini tactics** (inspired by Ancient Empires) | Medium–high among strategy fans | High | High | Differentiates RetroBox from collections made only of tiny arcade games, but requires maps and AI |

### Preliminary launch mix to test—not yet a final decision

A good first collection should demonstrate three different kinds of fun, not three versions of the same loop. The best preliminary combination is:

1. **One recognisable flagship:** the physics ball platformer.
2. **One fast arcade score-chaser:** the scrolling space shooter.
3. **One tiny pick-up-and-play game:** the precision tower stacker *or* a distinctive Snake variation.

Before committing, compare the list against real memories from the target audience—especially which Nokia/Java games people in Nigeria, the wider African market, and the diaspora actually remember and would replay. Do not assume global recognition equals local demand.

## 4. How we decide what goes into RetroBox

Score each candidate from 1–5 in these areas:

1. **Recall:** Do people recognise the game or its core mechanic immediately?
2. **Replayability:** Is there a reason to play again after a loss or completed run?
3. **Short-session fit:** Can a satisfying session happen in roughly 1–5 minutes?
4. **PWA fit:** Does it play well with touch controls, keyboard controls and portrait/landscape screens as appropriate?
5. **Build effort:** Can one developer produce a polished original version in a manageable amount of time?
6. **Content burden:** Does it need many levels, animations, dialogue, licensed teams or complex AI?
7. **Originality/rights risk:** Can we create a recognisably different game with original names, code, art, sound, level design and characters?

A strong first-release candidate should score well on recall, replayability and PWA fit, while keeping effort and content burden manageable. The final decision should include a small amount of audience research: ask people to rank a shortlist and, where possible, show them screenshots or short gameplay clips from the historical reference.

## 5. Rights and originality rule

RetroBox should treat old games as **research references**, not assume they are free to reproduce just because they are old or difficult to buy. In general, the underlying idea or broad gameplay mechanic is different from protected expression, but game code, artwork, music, characters, dialogue, stories, maps/levels and distinctive visual presentation can be protected; titles and logos may raise trademark issues. The exact position depends on jurisdiction and facts, so this is product guidance, not a legal opinion.

For every game we choose, use this workflow:

- Write down the core gameplay loop and what made it compelling.
- Design a new name, world, characters, visual language, sounds, user interface and level layouts.
- Write the implementation from scratch; do not copy source code or extracted assets.
- Avoid marketing the game as an official remake or implying affiliation with Nokia, Gameloft, EA, Sega or other rights holders.
- Investigate licensing if we ever decide that exact title, branding, characters, music or levels are essential to the product.

## 6. PWA and architecture implications

The research does **not** change the proposed stack:

- **React + TypeScript:** library, launch screen, game detail, settings and achievements UI.
- **Phaser:** 2D game runtime, sprites, animation, collision, game loops and input.
- **Vite + PWA plugin:** build system, installable web app, manifest and service-worker setup.
- **IndexedDB:** local saves, high scores, unlocked levels and game-specific progress.

Start with local-only play. Cache the application shell and the initial game assets for offline use. Any newly released game must be downloaded/cached on the device before it can be played offline. Do not add accounts, cloud saves, multiplayer, a backend, in-app currency or a live game store in the first version.

The game registry should be a simple list of metadata and launch components. Each game should own its gameplay code and assets but share a small set of app-wide services: audio settings, persistence, high-score reporting and pause/game-over conventions. Do not create a dynamic plugin framework or remote code loader.

## 8. Next research task

Do not start building three games just because the first examples came to mind. Continue the catalogue in this order:

1. Gather a **shortlist of 25–40 titles** and capture one screenshot or gameplay video per title.
2. Record the era, handset/platform, genre, controls, core loop, level/progression structure, likely build effort and possible original reinterpretation.
3. Compare titles across regions and communities, prioritising memories that recur across more than one source.
4. Choose the three launch concepts based on audience recall, variety and a solo developer's delivery capacity.
5. Only then update the main README and write a small game-specific design brief for each selected game.

**Current recommendation:** investigate **Bounce/Bounce Tales, Space Impact, Tower Bloxx, Diamond Rush, Gravity Defied, Snake, Rapid Roll and BrickBreaker/Block Breaker** first. They span different genres and difficulty levels, which makes them a useful first comparison set.
